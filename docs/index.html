In three sentences

  • Small drones are hard to see in cities: they are a few pixels wide, fast, and seen against buildings, trees and changing light.
  • An event camera ignores everything that is still, and reports brightness changes with microsecond timing, including the flicker of spinning propellers.
  • On 25 real test recordings, our event-based method found 70% of drones against 50% for the best normal-video AI, when both were allowed the same 5% false alarms. The gap was biggest for fast and very small drones, small racing drones, cluttered scenes and at night. Against plain sky and for slow drones the two were level, and at a stricter 1% false-alarm limit neither side won.

The problem

Why a drone is hard to spot

Picture a drone 300 m away. With a typical lens it covers about 10 pixels. In one video frame it looks a lot like a window corner, an antenna or a bird. If it moves fast, it smears across the frame. If the sun is behind it, the camera may wash it out completely.

Radio detectors help when the drone talks to its controller, but a drone flying a pre-set route can be radio-silent. That leaves cameras as an important layer, so it matters which kind of camera works best.

The sensor

What is an event camera?

A normal camera takes a full picture many times a second, whether anything changed or not. An event camera works pixel by pixel. Each pixel watches its own brightness and sends a tiny message, an event, only when that brightness changes by more than a set amount. An event is just four numbers: where (x, y), when (t, to the microsecond) and which way (p, brighter or darker).

Three things follow. A wall or a building that does not move sends nothing. A fast object does not blur. A propeller, whose blades sweep past the same pixels hundreds of times a second, makes those pixels flicker at a steady rhythm. That rhythm is a fingerprint birds do not have.

The method

How PropPulse finds a drone

  1. Find blobs of activity

    Every 10 ms, count events in small squares of the image and mark busy clusters as candidates. Areas that are always busy, such as trees in the wind, are noted as clutter.

  2. Follow each blob

    Link candidates over time into tracks, so we know how each object moves. Every track also carries its own small bank of spiking “tuning-fork” neurons that react to each incoming event (see below).

  3. Hold the drone still, then listen for the propeller

    Shift the events to cancel the drone's own motion, as if a camera were following it. Then count events in tiny cells 4,000 times a second and look for a steady rhythm between 70 and 1,000 Hz, plus its multiples. Fake rhythms caused by fast motion across the pixel grid, and flickering lamps or LED signs, are removed.

  4. Ask a trained checker

    Combine the rhythm and the neurons' responses with simple facts about the track (speed, how smooth its path is, how unusual it is for that spot) and let a small trained model decide: drone or not. Simple rules then drop shadows and duplicate tracks of the same drone.

We also trained a standard AI detector (YOLO11n) on "event images": each 33 ms of events is drawn as a three-channel picture showing brighter changes, darker changes, and how recently each pixel fired.

Keeping score

A fair race: same scenes, same false-alarm rate

Comparing detectors is easy to get wrong. A detector that shouts "drone!" at everything will find every drone, and also every bird and window. So we fix how often each method is allowed to raise a false alarm (1% or 5% of 0.2-second time slots) and then ask how many real drones it finds. We call that the detection rate.

We use FRED, a public dataset recorded with an event camera and a normal camera side by side, so both methods see exactly the same scenes. To mimic drones farther away, we also shrink the images by 2× and 4×.

Results

Where events help, and where they don't

Final round: 25 test recordings. Each bar compares the event method with the better of two normal-camera AIs (a 3-frame network and YOLO11n) in that situation. Overall, the event method found 20 percentage points more drones than the best normal-camera AI. If the experiment were repeated on other recordings, that gap would very likely stay positive: the 95% range is +12 to +28 points.
Event camera + PropPulseNormal camera + best AIDetection rate at 5% false alarms

Events win where normal cameras struggle: fast motion (no blur), tiny drones, cluttered scenes with trees and ground, and darkness. Against plain sky a drone is easy to see in a normal photo, and there the two were level. Slow drones were also a draw. When we made false alarms rarer (1% instead of 5%), no method was clearly ahead. A real system would likely use both cameras.

A surprise: feeding events to a standard AI detector as pictures (event YOLO11n) did not beat the same detector on normal video (49% vs 44%, within chance). It was especially poor for tiny, distant drones. How you process events matters as much as the camera.

One more honest finding: most of the event method's advantage comes from how cleanly events show moving things against still clutter. The propeller rhythm helps, but it is the second clue, not the first.

A bonus

Propellers have fingerprints

The event camera measured a blade-pass frequency for each drone. The bigger DJI propellers spin slower than the small racing-drone propellers, so the frequency hints at the drone's size, from a single passive camera. One racing drone (Cineape20) varied a lot (about 200 to 850 Hz) as it changed throttle.

Spiking neurons

Listening for propellers with spiking neurons

Our rhythm check waits for a quarter of a second of data and then runs a frequency analysis. Brains do it differently. A resonate-and-fire neuron behaves like a tuning fork: every event gives it a small push, and only the neuron tuned to the propeller's rhythm builds up enough to fire.

On 71,000 real track windows, a bank of 48 such neurons told drones from clutter about as well as the frequency analysis (score 0.68 vs 0.69), and better when the two were combined (0.76). On drones it fired after a median of 37 ms, compared with 210 ms for the frequency analysis, and used about 19× fewer operations. It also fires on 40% of non-drone windows, so a spike is a hint, not a verdict. In the final detector every track runs such a bank on every event, and its responses help the trained checker decide. Operation counts are estimates; energy was not measured.

Being clear about limits

Limits of this study

Speed and power

Our event method spots 62% of drones within a quarter of a second, more than the normal-camera YOLO (50%), but its median time to first detection (0.12 s) is still slower than YOLO's single frame (0.03 s), because weaker tracks wait for the rhythm to be measured. On one laptop CPU core it needs 0.1 s of processing per second of a typical recording; a scene where most of the image sways with the camera mount is still about 5× slower than real time. Low power is only estimated, not measured on real chips. And a 5% false-alarm rate is far too high for a real deployment: these results rank approaches, they are not a finished system.

Real cities

Paired data comes from one dataset, one city and five drone models, with a fixed camera. Moving or vibrating cameras, traffic, LED billboards and long drone-free recordings were not tested. "Distance" is imitated by shrinking images, which flatters the event method; only two test recordings were at night.
